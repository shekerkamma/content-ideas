# Chapter 13: Competition, the crash case and stop rules
*Whitepaper §12–12.2 and §14.2*

## Core Idea
Incumbents cannot copy the model because their three possible answers are blocked by cost structure and law. The real danger is internal: getting stuck in a protected corner or loving one's own forecast, which is why a crash case travels with every forecast and stop rules are written in advance with dates.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Who the competitor is, by market**:
  - Commercial: TI, Analog Devices, Renesas, Microchip, ST. They win on range, distribution, software tools and trust; conceded. Deepgrid serves a demand shape their costs exclude: a 10,000-unit programme with a specific requirement and screening.
  - Defence: not a chip company winning on merit but an import the law is removing; the real rival is another Indian company, separated by who has finished silicon and a screening process first.
  - Drones: Qualcomm QRB5165, nothing else integrated (Track B).
- **Three blocked competitor answers**:
  1. **Cut the price**: the barrier is recovering setup cost on a small programme, not unit price; after a PIL date price is irrelevant.
  2. **Open a low-cost Indian design centre**: lowers engineering cost but not the $2–5 M per-chip layout and mask cost, and does not make the chip Indian-designed for DAP-2020.
  3. **Buy the new company**: the likely ending and acceptable for this round, but not a plan to depend on.
- **The low-end entry trap, said plainly**: entrants at the protected low end often stay dependent on the protection and get squeezed when it ends. Escape route: the silicon-tested block library and the node roadmap (90/55/45 nm from 2028, driven by channel count and DSP throughput), both portable to unprotected markets.
- **The crash case travels with the plan**: re-price FY31 as if Chinese chips flood India, and note which row the crash cannot touch.
- **Stop rules**: decisions written in advance, with dates, about what happens when a specific bad thing occurs, so they cannot be renegotiated "in an excited meeting".
- **The Munger self-audit**: the plan was checked for wishful thinking with Charlie Munger's checklist; the core survived (10x cost is arithmetic, ₹2.88 Cr is fact, import bans are real) and four biases were fixed:
  - **Borrowed glory**: others' order books sounded like Deepgrid's → labelled as door size; money moves only on signed letters.
  - **Permission mistaken for orders** → the three buyer clocks (ch12).
  - **Protecting our own number**: the forecast summed to exactly ₹1,000 Cr in every revision → the crash table now travels with it.
  - **One skill mistaken for another**: chip skill is not customer-support skill → support engineers are a funded line.
- **When everything pushes one way**: national pride, deadlines, finished documents and tender numbers all push "build more, faster"; unanimity of forces is itself the warning. Defence: the rule, the scoreboard, the stop rules.

## Key Concepts
- **S1 (meter letter)**: if Ripple or another meter maker has not signed by chip 2's factory-order date, chip 2 waits a full cycle and its money moves to chips 1 and 3. No exceptions.
- **S2 (screening)**: if chip 6 fails military screening twice, every document moves defence revenue out by twelve months, within thirty days.
- **S3 (price floor)**: in FY29 check Chinese prices properly (duties and tender rules included); if below Deepgrid's cost to make, exit the fan market and keep e-scooters plus own products. Compute the walk-away price this quarter.
- **S4 (SCL delay)**: if SCL Mohali runs more than two cycles late, say so publicly and lean on the other two factories.
- **Homework**: establish real FY29 Chinese chip prices in India before an investor asks.
- **Meter row fails loudest**: if no letter by the factory-order date, ₹480 Cr moves out a year and the plan still closes near ₹520 Cr.

## Mental Models
- Think of **the crash case as the cure for loving your own forecast**.
- Use **"which row can the crash not reach?"**: import-banned defence revenue.
- Treat **a forecast that never changes across revisions as a smell**.

## Anti-patterns
- **Claiming to beat incumbents on merit in open commercial markets**.
- **Depending on acquisition as the exit plan**.
- **Setting stop criteria during the crisis** instead of in advance.
- **Treating unanimous enthusiasm as confirmation**.

## Worked Example
**Applying stop rule S1 on a real date (reconstructed decision):**
1. Trigger date: the day chip 2's production factory order is due.
2. Check: is there a signed letter from Ripple or another meter maker? Suppose no.
3. Action, pre-agreed: chip 2 waits one full cycle (~198 days); its allocated money moves to chips 1 and 3.
4. Forecast consequence, pre-computed: the ₹480 Cr meter row shifts out a year; the other rows close near ₹520 Cr.
5. What not to do: extend the deadline because the latest meeting "felt close". The rule exists precisely for that meeting.

## Key Takeaways
1. Concede open commercial markets to incumbents; compete where their cost base and the law exclude them.
2. All three incumbent responses are blocked; acquisition is acceptable, not a plan.
3. Escape the low-end trap through the block library and channel-count-driven node moves.
4. Publish a crash case next to every forecast.
5. Write stop rules with dates before they are needed, and audit the plan for wishful thinking.

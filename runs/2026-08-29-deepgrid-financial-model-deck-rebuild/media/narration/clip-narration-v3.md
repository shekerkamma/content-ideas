# Clip narration — the memory spectrum

**landscape** · 57.9 s · Data placement is an architectural choice
Six chips, and six answers to the same question: who schedules the movement of data, and where does that data live. On the left is our own SoC1.1 as it runs today — eight cores queueing behind a single DMA engine. Next, Mobileye's EyeQ, where VLIW cores share an L2 through a multi-channel DMA: better than one engine, still memory-shared. Then NVIDIA's Orin, which answers the question with brute force — two thousand and forty-eight CUDA cores and per-streaming-multiprocessor shared memory, at sixty watts. At the far right, Tesla's Dojo D1: three hundred and fifty-four cores, each with its own private SRAM and no DRAM on the die at all. The most extreme position on the spectrum, and a project Tesla disbanded in twenty twenty-five. Hailo sits near it with a distributed memory fabric and no external DRAM, extremely efficient across a narrow band of workloads. Now watch the middle. Per-core DMA against a shared level-two cache — an engine for every core, memory still shared — is where the roadmap goes, and it is where most of the industry already sits.
